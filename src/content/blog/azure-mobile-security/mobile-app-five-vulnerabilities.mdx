The app works. Customers are ordering. Reviews are fine. Then somebody runs a security scan, and the report comes back with five findings.

Here's the uncomfortable bit: nobody on the team made a mistake. Every one of these is a default, a leftover from testing, or a line copied from a sample. None of them would survive a code review, because nobody ever wrote them on purpose.

## What are the five findings?

| # | Finding | Severity | What an attacker needs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Secret in the app package | High | A copy of your app |
| 2 | Session token in plain storage | High | The phone, rooted, or a backup |
| 3 | Endpoint with no auth | **Critical** | curl and a URL |
| 4 | TLS certificate check disabled | High | The same coffee-shop wifi |
| 5 | Permissions the app never uses | Medium | A compromise of the app first |

## 1. A secret in the app package

An API key in `strings.xml`, `Info.plist`, a `.env` bundled into assets, or a constant in your code. An `.apk` or `.ipa` is a zip file; anything inside is public.

**Fix:** the app holds a short-lived user token, your API holds the keys, and Key Vault holds them at rest. The full walkthrough is [the first post in this series](../mobile-secret-in-apk).

## 2. The session token in plain storage

`SharedPreferences` and `UserDefaults` are plain files. On a rooted or jailbroken phone — or straight out of a device backup — they're readable.

**Fix:** Android Keystore or `EncryptedSharedPreferences`, and the iOS Keychain. In .NET MAUI, use `SecureStorage`, not `Preferences`. They're one word apart and completely different.

## 3. An endpoint with no auth

Usually `/admin`, a `/health` route that happily returns config, `/debug`, or an internal route someone added to test and never locked down.

**Fix:** make authorization the default and opt *out* explicitly, not the other way round:

```csharp
builder.Services.AddAuthorizationBuilder()
    .SetFallbackPolicy(new AuthorizationPolicyBuilder()
        .RequireAuthenticatedUser().Build());
```

Now a route you forget about fails closed instead of open.

## 4. TLS certificate checking disabled

Someone switched it off to test against a local server with a self-signed cert, and it shipped. Every request is now readable by anyone on the same network.

**Fix:** remove the override, and wrap any dev-only handler in `#if DEBUG` so it physically can't end up in a release build.

## 5. Permissions the app never uses

Contacts, location, camera, storage — copied in from a tutorial. They lower install rates, fail store review more often, and widen what an attacker gets if the app is ever compromised.

**Fix:** delete every permission, then add back only the ones that break.

## Which one should you fix first?

Number 3, and it isn't close. Findings 1, 2 and 4 need somebody to have your app, your phone or your network. An endpoint with no auth needs nothing — it's reachable from anywhere on earth right now, and bots are already scanning for it (see [the post on automated attacks](../ai-bot-attacks-mobile-app)).

## How do you check your own app today?

<Callout type="tip" title="A 20-minute self-scan">
  - Unzip your APK and grep it for `key`, `secret`, `password`, `Bearer` and `http://`.
  - Sign in, then look at what the app wrote to disk.
  - List your API routes and mark which ones require a token.
  - Diff the permissions in your manifest against the ones your code actually calls.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- The five common findings are defaults or testing leftovers, which is exactly why they reach production.
- An unauthenticated endpoint is the top priority because exploiting it needs no access to the device or network.
- `SharedPreferences` and `UserDefaults` aren't secure storage — use Keystore/Keychain, or `SecureStorage` in .NET MAUI.
- A fallback authorization policy makes a forgotten route fail closed.
- Audit manifest permissions against what the app really uses, and remove the rest.
